## THE PROBLEM (TOM, from play)

"It's a bit silly to present all the Spanish numbers in a row, then do match pairs. Simply doing sessions on 1-10 would have to be a turn-off for most people. It's less knowing the counting. If we add in how numbers show up, that would be valuable: paying, people in a reservation, time, hearing costs, hearing time, stops on a bus or train, number of drinks. But let's not put too much scenario in too early."

## THE DIAGNOSIS

A traveler almost never needs to SAY a number from one to ten (fingers work; the words are near-cognates). What fails on a trip is HEARING a number at speed: a price over a counter, a time, a platform. Numbers are an ear skill; counting is the raw material. The shipped kit shape (a card per number, a words board, rungs) trains the wrong thing.

## THE STRATEGY (TOM: "yes, this all feels right")

**1. Met as a set, practised by ear.** No card-by-card parade. One card for the whole set, then the keypad.

**2. Three layers, one per chapter, mirroring the chapter flow:**

| Chapter | What | Why here |
|---|---|---|
| 1 · The words | one to ten + the bills (veinte, cincuenta, cien) | the base layer; the bills are what is in your hand |
| 2 · Your first sentences | THE NUMBER MACHINE: the tens (veinte … noventa) as fillers, the frame "___ y ___"; once to quince ride in as five words. 88 = ochenta y ocho lives here | Spanish numbers are built by a frame, exactly like the rooms: about 25 words + one frame reach 999 |
| 3 · Sentences by place | the forms numbers take in the wild, each inside its own session: prices with cents (doce con cincuenta) in Paying, clock times in Telling time, room and platform numbers in Checking in / Metro; hundreds arrive here (177 = ciento setenta y siete) | each needs words chapter 1 does not own (a las, somos, euros, con), per the context rule |

**3. The bar (TOM):** SAY to 100, UNDERSTAND to 999. A traveler hears large numbers constantly and says them almost never. Production work stays small; the effort goes to the ear.

**4. Chapter 1 scenarios stay minimal (TOM: "not too much scenario too early"):** only "how many" (Dos, por favor · ¿Dos? No, tres — chat's contexts, already in the pack) and a heard price as a bare number. Time, party size, stops, cents wait for chapter 3.

**5. One numbers session in chapter 1, not two** (Code's lean; Tom has not objected): the set of ten plus the three bills, about four minutes, mostly ear.

## THE EXERCISE FAMILY: one keypad, three stimuli, two lengths

The keypad is ONE surface (digits in a fixed place the thumb learns), so all the difficulty stays in decoding Spanish.

| Form | Stimulus → response | Mode |
|---|---|---|
| Hear it, tap the digit | you hear "siete", tap 7 | sound on; the core |
| The run | three numbers in succession, then tap all three (3, 5, 8) | both modes; the lap |
| The reverse keypad | a digit is shown, the KEYS are the words, tap "siete"; with sound on the key speaks when tapped | both modes; the saying side |

**Session order (proposal):** the set card → heard singles (two or three reps, the introduction) → the numeral-to-word board (7 ↔ siete, so it is not the board every other session has) → the reverse keypad → chat's "how many" contexts on the shipped rungs → runs of three as the lap.

## THE SOUND-OFF TWIN (TOM raised it; ruled together)

What audio trains is not sound as such: a spoken number is there ONCE and then gone. The silent form copies that property.

- **A single flashed word is a gimmick and is OUT (TOM: "flashing one number might be super easy").** Reading a Spanish number is far easier than hearing one; the eye takes a short word in a fifth of a second. No flash closes that gap.
- **The silent form is THE RUN: three words in succession (TOM).** Each word replaces the last in the SAME spot, about two words a second (roughly how fast numbers are said), then the keypad. Not all three at once: that becomes re-reading. Succession forces in-order processing, the way speech does.
- **The difficulty must be about Spanish, not about the flash.** Runs are chosen from the NEIGHBORS that really trip people: seis / siete · dos / diez · cinco / cincuenta · later sesenta / setenta (which catches fluent speakers). Not random numbers.
- **Adaptive, never punishing:** the pace tightens and the run grows to four as the learner clears them; it eases on a miss. "Show it again" is always there (where "Tap to hear it again" sits) and records as a supported rep, never a fail. No countdown, no speed score. The pressure is the word leaving, which is how the real thing works.
- **Singles survive only with sound on**, as the first two or three introduction reps. The silent mode skips them. "I can't listen right now" swaps heard runs for flashed runs for the session.
- **The scattered field (many digits on screen, find them fast) is PARKED:** the hard part becomes visual search, not Spanish. The keypad already is a field of digits. Revisit only if runs feel flat at play.
- **Said plainly:** sound-off will always be the weaker numbers workout. The goal for the silent twin is "still useful", not "just as good".

## THE GIMMICK CHECK (TOM: "make sure the flash actually makes it slightly harder and not a gimmick")

The app records time and accuracy per exercise. Tom's first play is the test: if every run clears at the fastest pace without effort, it is too easy and it is tuned or cut. Decide at play, not on paper.
