## B. WHAT THE NUMBERS SAY (three findings, not one)

1. **The machines are the right length and the wrong sequence.** Each is in band; seven in a row, each on one frame for its whole run, is the repetition. The depth ruling sized a machine well in isolation and nobody composed the sequence.
2. **The kit lessons are the length problem, and they are batched by exercise type.** 13-item lessons run ten minutes, and the composer groups the same exercise back to back: every present card first, every MC second, then the fills, then thirteen cold typings, then six lap reps. That is where "the exercises are too repetitive" is literally true, and it happens before the learner meets a single machine.
3. **The machines carry no context yet.** Their narrative cues exist ("The glass is empty · Ask for"), but between machine sessions there is no scenario for them to land in; the first table lesson comes after all seven.

## C. THE PROPOSAL (Tom's direction, with minutes)

Rooms of TWO machines per lesson, each machine on a short arc; the rest of a machine's drilling (lap 2, the stretch, exchanges beyond the first) moves to its returns (the machine shop, the return door's lap, the scenes). Kit lessons split into two each, so no session carries more than ~7 new phrases. Interleave rooms with kit and scenario lessons so no two consecutive sessions share a shape.

**A room lesson (two machines):** for each machine, drill (forge-present + conveyor lap) 62s · weld ×3 48s · exchange ×2 16s = 2.1 min; two machines 4.2 min + close 14s = **~4.5 min**, ~15 beats, and the frame changes halfway through. Not tiny; punchy.

**A split kit lesson (6-7 items) at today's shape:** ~4.7-5.5 min. If the composer interleaves per item instead of batching by type (present → its MC → its fill → its cold rep, in waves of 2-3 items), the same lesson stops feeling like thirteen of the same thing. That is a composer change and a chat ruling (the lesson-breath canvas owns sequencing).
