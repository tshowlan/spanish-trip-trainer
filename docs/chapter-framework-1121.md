**Scenes are not a chapter.** They are the form review takes: a composed visit to a place, made of the learner's own due lines in the order they happen. No cast; the other speaker is a role; the learner's trip is the story (ruled 11/20, Tom aligned).

## THE TESTS (ask them in this order)

1. **Is it a single word or sign?** Chapter 1.
2. **Does the trip go wrong without it?** Chapter 2. (You do not get the thing, you cannot pay, you eat what you must not, you do not understand what stands between you and the thing.) Safety lines are chapter 2 whatever else is true.
3. **Is it one line, with a short answer?** Chapter 3. It makes the trip more yours or more gracious, and the trip is fine without it.
4. **Does it take several turns, each depending on the last?** Chapter 4.

Chapter 3's moves. The first three exist only where there is a choice (the Counter, the Table, the Shop; never the taxi, never Help). The fourth, courtesy, can happen anywhere:
- **How I want it:** sin azúcar, con poco hielo, poco hecho.
- **A simple question:** ¿Qué me recomienda? · ¿Es dulce? · ¿Qué es esto?
- **For whom:** Para ella, una copa de vino.
- **The kind no, the kind word, and the small courtesy:** Estoy lleno, gracias. · Todo muy rico. · Buenas noches. · the bigger thank-yous and the superlatives (Muchas gracias, muchísimas gracias, buenísimo; Tom, 11/21), taught as two patterns the learner can reuse: muy + a word, and the -ísimo ending. · asking where to throw something away (Tom's note 9: a straw wrapper in your hand, not a bin at your lodging).

Chapter 4's unit is the task. **A task earns a place only where English runs out** (Tom, 11/21): the hotel desk is where a traveler meets the most English, so it gets nothing, and big ticket desks are probably the same. Candidates that pass, to be confirmed against what Tom actually met: a table for tonight · something is wrong (the order, the bill) · trying something on in a small shop · the pharmacy. Tickets and times is doubtful and stays off the list until a traveler reports needing it. The question words chapter 2 held back (¿Cuál? ¿Cómo? ¿Cuándo? and the rest) arrive inside tasks, with what to listen for in the answer. Tengo as a frame (Tengo una reserva) arrives here too, with the table task.

**Amendment to 11/20 Part A:** the hotel desk is neither a chapter 2 place nor a chapter 4 task. Reception speaks English; the app teaches for the places that do not. The ride pick-up line stays chapter 2 and joins the Street batch.

## NEW SENTENCE STRUCTURES (added 2026-11-24; re-scoped the same day on Tom's word)

The app prepares a traveler for what they will meet. It does not teach every way to say a thing. So a new sentence structure is a decision made on purpose, and this section is where it is recorded. It is a register, not a debt: nothing here has to be built on.

**Scope (Tom, 11/24): this is about sentence structure, not vocabulary.** A structure is a way of building a sentence the learner has not used: a tense, a command, a new shape. A new verb or adjective inside a shape the learner already owns is vocabulary, and the card rules govern it, not this section.

**Before a line brings a new structure, in this order:**
1. **Does a structure the learner owns do the same job?** Then use that. A different job (saying something is missing, against ordering a new one) is a different job.
2. **If not, is the phrase worth owning by itself?** Then it is taught as a fixed phrase on a card, never as grammar, and logged below with the reason.
3. **A later chapter may reuse a logged structure where its own content calls for it.** No chapter is written to justify one. A one-off may stay a one-off for good.

A structure the learner only hears is logged too. It is owed recognition, and at most one sentence in a note tying it to the verb the learner already says.

| Structure | In chapter 2 | Why it is here | Later use, if any |
|---|---|---|---|
| The past: he + -ado / -ido | said once: Ya he terminado. Heard: ¿Ha terminado?, ¿Se ha caído? | Nothing the learner owns says "I'm done" (trip notes 21 and 26). A confident one-off | Chapter 4's "something is wrong" task would reuse it if that task is written. Not required |
| The thing after the verb: Falta ___ | said: Falta un vaso (card, three lines) | It says something is missing from what was ordered or laid, which is a different job from ordering a new one (Tom, 11/24) | None needed |
| The command: Llame a ___ | said: two lines (la policía · un médico). Heard: Llame al 112 | Safety. It tells a bystander what to do, and the same word comes back at the learner | None needed |
| The short request: ¿Me ___? | said: ¿Me pone ___? (two lines), ¿Me cobra? (three lines) | The bar's own shape in Spain, the short form of ¿Me puede ___? The learner hears its other half (¿Qué le pongo?) | None needed |
| The we-form | said: Queremos (two lines). In a note: hemos | Ordering for the table (premise P2, Tom's trip) | None needed |
| Heard: quiere / quieren | ¿Quiere copia?, ¿Quieren postre o café? | The waiter's side of Quiero; answered with an owned Sí or No | The third turn, already planned |
| Heard: the staff's le / lo + verb (traigo, pongo, retiro, digo, pregunto, miro) | replies only | The answers to the learner's own asks | None. Recognition only |

**Not rows, by the scope rule:** pedir, comer, pago, es, lleva, tengo, soy, como, no entiendo, and Está bien así. Each is a word or a fixed phrase inside a shape the learner owns (an I-statement, a question about a thing, an owned ask). Code listed them at v650 and chat graded them; they leave the register as vocabulary.

Code adds a row only when a line brings a tense, a command, or a sentence shape that no earlier line has.
